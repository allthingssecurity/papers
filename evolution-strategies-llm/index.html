Evolution Strategies at Scale: LLM Fine-Tuning Beyond Reinforcement Learning

Xin Qiu, Yulu Gan, Conor F. Hayes, Qiyao Liang, Yinggan Xu, Roberto Dailey, Elliot Meyerson, Babak Hodjat, Risto Miikkulainen

2025 · Cognizant AI Lab & MIT & UT Austin

The first successful application of Evolution Strategies to full-parameter fine-tuning of billion-parameter LLMs — outperforming PPO and GRPO in accuracy, stability, sample efficiency, and resistance to reward hacking, all without backpropagation.

Why Should You Care?

Right now, making AI models smarter after their initial training almost always uses a technique called Reinforcement Learning (RL). Think of RL like training a dog: you watch every action, then reward or punish each one.

This paper shows there's a radically different approach: Evolution Strategies (ES). Instead of watching every action, you create 30 slightly different copies of the AI, test them all, and keep the traits of the best performers. Like natural selection, but for neural networks.

The shocking result? This "evolutionary" approach beats the standard RL methods across the board — and it doesn't even need to compute gradients (the mathematical backbone of modern deep learning).

The Big Idea: Evolve, Don't Backpropagate

Imagine you're trying to find the highest point on a mountain range, but you're blindfolded:

RL Approach (PPO/GRPO)

You feel the slope under your feet at each tiny step and walk uphill. But if the terrain is jagged, you keep stumbling into local traps.

ES Approach (This Paper)

You scatter 30 friends randomly around you, they all report their altitude, and you move toward the highest reports. The jagged terrain gets smoothed out by the crowd's average.

ES dominated on every single model tested — from tiny 0.5B to large 8B parameter models, across two different model families (Qwen and Llama). Perhaps most remarkably, it used less than 20% of the training samples that RL methods needed.

Why Doesn't the AI "Cheat"?

A major problem with RL fine-tuning is reward hacking — the AI finds weird loopholes to get high scores without actually being helpful. For example, when asked to be concise, RL-trained models sometimes output meaningless symbols to minimize length.

ES naturally avoids this because it doesn't optimize a single model intensely. Instead, it evaluates a population of models, and extreme, hacky behaviors average out. It's like how a committee decision is usually more sensible than one person's extreme opinion.

Key Takeaway

You can make large language models significantly smarter by evolving their weights rather than computing gradients — and it works better, is more stable, uses fewer samples, and resists gaming the reward. This opens an entirely new paradigm for AI training that requires only inference, no backpropagation.

Test Your Understanding

Q1: How does ES explore the solution space?

ES creates N=30 slightly different versions of the model by adding random noise to all parameters simultaneously. Each copy is tested, and the original model is updated toward the noise patterns that produced the best results.

Q2: What is "reward hacking"?

Reward hacking occurs when the model exploits loopholes in the reward function. For example, GRPO-trained models sometimes output meaningless symbol strings to maximize conciseness rewards, rather than producing genuinely concise answers. ES naturally avoids this.

Q3: How many perturbed models does ES need per iteration?

One of the paper's most surprising findings is that a population of just N=30 suffices, even for models with billions of parameters. Previous theoretical predictions suggested quadratic scaling with dimensionality, but LLMs' intrinsic low-dimensional structure may explain this.

How It Works: The ES Pipeline

The ES fine-tuning loop has three phases repeated for \(T\) iterations:

Phase 1: Perturbation

Sample \(N=30\) random noise vectors from a standard Gaussian distribution. Each noise vector has the same dimensionality as the entire model (billions of parameters). Rather than storing these huge vectors, only the random seeds are saved — the noise can be regenerated identically from the seed.

Phase 2: Evaluation (Massively Parallel)

For each noise vector, perturb the model's weights in-place, run inference on the task, compute a reward, then restore the original weights. Each of the 30 evaluations is independent and can run on a separate GPU.

Phase 3: Update

Z-score normalize the rewards, then update parameters as a weighted sum of noise vectors scaled by their rewards. This moves the model toward the noise patterns that correlated with high performance.

Core Equations

The ES Gradient Estimator

The key theoretical insight: ES is actually computing an unbiased estimate of the gradient of a Gaussian-smoothed fitness function:

$$ \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})} \left[ F(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \sigma \boldsymbol{\epsilon}) \right] = \frac{1}{\sigma} \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})} \left[ F(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \sigma \boldsymbol{\epsilon}) \, \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \right] $$
\(F\) fitness/reward function \(\theta\) model parameters (billions) \(\sigma\) noise scale = 0.001 \(\epsilon\) Gaussian noise vector

This means ES is not random search — it's performing principled gradient estimation through random function evaluations. The Gaussian smoothing acts as a convolution kernel that softens jagged reward landscapes.

Parameter Update Rule

$$ \boldsymbol{\theta}_t \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} + \alpha \cdot \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \hat{R}_n \, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_n $$
\(\alpha\) learning rate = 5×10-4 \(N\) population size = 30 \(\hat{R}_n\) z-score normalized reward \(\epsilon_n\) n-th noise vector

Z-Score Reward Normalization

$$ \hat{R}_n = \frac{R_n - \bar{R}}{\text{std}(R_1, \ldots, R_N)} $$
\(\bar{R}\) mean reward in population \(\text{std}\) standard deviation

Normalizing rewards within each generation ensures consistent update magnitudes regardless of absolute reward scale.

Parameter-Space vs. Action-Space Exploration

This is the conceptual crux of why ES works differently from RL:

ES: Parameter-Space

Noise is injected into the weights once. A single perturbation affects the entire response trajectory coherently. This is equivalent to Gaussian smoothing of the reward landscape.

RL: Action-Space

Noise is injected at each token via sampling. Each token decision is independently noisy. Credit assignment must trace rewards back through hundreds of tokens.

Test Your Understanding

Q1: Why is ES actually performing principled optimization, not random search?

The key theoretical result is that the expectation of F(θ + σε)·ε / σ equals the gradient of the Gaussian-smoothed fitness. So ES is doing gradient descent, just estimating the gradient via function evaluations instead of backpropagation.

Q2: How does ES handle the memory challenge of storing 30 noise vectors the size of the entire model?

This is one of the seven scalability techniques: rather than storing N full noise tensors (which would require N copies of model memory), only 30 integer seeds are stored. The identical noise vectors can be regenerated deterministically from these seeds when needed.

Q3: Why is ES naturally resistant to reward hacking?

ES optimizes over a distribution of solutions (population of 30), not a single model. Reward hacking exploits specific, narrow loopholes that a single model might find, but these don't survive averaging across a diverse population. Additionally, ES uses no explicit KL penalty — the population-based smoothing provides natural regularization.

Q4: What does "zeroth-order optimization" mean?

In optimization theory, "order" refers to what derivatives you use: zeroth-order = just function values (f(x)), first-order = gradients (∇f), second-order = Hessians (∇²f). ES is zeroth-order because it only evaluates F(θ+σε) — it never computes ∇F.

Technical Deep Dive: Natural Evolution Strategies

ES can be derived from the REINFORCE trick applied to a Gaussian search distribution over parameters. Consider the search distribution \(\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})\). We want to maximize:

$$ J(\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta} \sim \pi} \left[ F(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right] = \int F(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}) \, d\boldsymbol{\theta} $$
\(J\) expected fitness \(\mu\) mean of search distribution \(\pi\) Gaussian search distribution

Applying the log-derivative trick (score function estimator):

$$ \begin{aligned} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} J &= \int F(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, d\boldsymbol{\theta} \\ &= \int F(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \log \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, d\boldsymbol{\theta} \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta} \sim \pi} \left[ F(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \log \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right] \end{aligned} $$

For \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sigma \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\), \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), we get \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \log \pi = \frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{\sigma}\), yielding the ES gradient estimator:

$$ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} J = \frac{1}{\sigma} \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})} \left[ F(\boldsymbol{\mu} + \sigma \boldsymbol{\epsilon}) \, \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \right] $$

This shows ES is equivalent to REINFORCE where the "policy" is over parameters rather than actions, and the "actions" are weight perturbations rather than token selections.

Gaussian Smoothing Interpretation

The objective \(J(\boldsymbol{\mu})\) can be rewritten as a convolution:

$$ J(\boldsymbol{\mu}) = (F * \mathcal{G}_\sigma)(\boldsymbol{\mu}) $$
\(*\) convolution operator \(\mathcal{G}_\sigma\) Gaussian kernel with bandwidth \(\sigma\)

This means ES is not optimizing the raw fitness \(F\) but a smoothed version \(F * \mathcal{G}_\sigma\). For small \(\sigma\), this closely approximates \(F\) while eliminating sharp local optima. For the conciseness task, where GRPO's reward landscape has deceptive local optima (reward hacking), ES's Gaussian smoothing naturally regularizes against these.

Why N=30 Suffices: Intrinsic Dimensionality

Vemula et al. (2019) predicted sample complexity \(N = O(d^2)\) where \(d\) is the parameter count. For \(d = 10^9\), this would require \(N \sim 10^{18}\) — clearly infeasible. The paper's \(N=30\) success suggests the intrinsic dimensionality of the LLM fine-tuning landscape is dramatically lower than the ambient dimensionality.

This is consistent with findings in the LoRA literature that fine-tuning operates in a low-rank subspace. ES may be implicitly discovering and exploiting this structure through its z-score normalization, which acts as a form of dimensionality-adaptive search.

Implementation: Scalable ES Algorithm

import torch import numpy as np class EvolutionStrategies: def __init__(self, model, N=30, sigma=0.001, lr=5e-4): self.model = model self.N = N self.sigma = sigma self.lr = lr def step(self, reward_fn, prompts): seeds = [np.random.randint(0, 2**32) for _ in range(self.N)] rewards = [] # Phase 1-2: Perturb & Evaluate (parallelizable) for seed in seeds: self._apply_noise(seed, scale=self.sigma) R = reward_fn(self.model, prompts) # greedy decode rewards.append(R) self._apply_noise(seed, scale=-self.sigma) # restore # Phase 3: Z-score normalize & update rewards = np.array(rewards) rewards = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / (rewards.std() + 1e-8) for n, seed in enumerate(seeds): # Decomposed update: layer-by-layer, seed-by-seed rng = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed) for p in self.model.parameters(): noise = torch.randn_like(p, generator=rng) p.data += self.lr / self.N * rewards[n] * noise def _apply_noise(self, seed, scale): rng = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed) for p in self.model.parameters(): p.data += scale * torch.randn_like(p, generator=rng)

Open Questions

  • Theoretical sample complexity: Why does \(N=30\) work? The gap between \(O(d^2)\) theory and \(N=30\) practice is enormous. Formal analysis of ES in the presence of low intrinsic dimensionality is needed.
  • Hybrid ES-RL: Could parameter-space and action-space exploration be combined? E.g., ES for coarse exploration, RL for fine-grained refinement.
  • Adaptive \(\sigma\) scheduling: The paper uses fixed \(\sigma = 0.001\). Could annealing or adaptive bandwidth improve convergence?
  • Non-isotropic covariance: Full NES uses learned covariance. Could CMA-ES-style covariance adaptation improve ES for LLMs?
  • Process rewards: ES currently only handles outcome-level rewards. Can it be extended to process-level (per-token) reward signals?
  • Pretraining with ES: If ES works for fine-tuning, could it work for training from scratch? The follow-up paper "Hyperscale ES" explores this for RWKV.

Test Your Understanding

Q1: The ES gradient estimator is derived from which technique?

The ES gradient estimator uses the log-derivative trick (also called the score function estimator or REINFORCE trick): ∇ E[F] = E[F · ∇ log π]. For Gaussian π, ∇ log π = ε/σ, yielding the ES formula. This is the same trick used in REINFORCE for RL, but applied to parameter space instead of action space.

Q2: Why does Gaussian smoothing help with reward hacking?

Reward hacking typically exploits narrow, sharp peaks in the reward landscape — degenerate solutions that score very high in a tiny region of parameter space. Gaussian smoothing (convolution with G_σ) acts as a low-pass filter that eliminates these narrow peaks while preserving broad, robust optima. The smoothed objective J = F * G_σ literally cannot represent features sharper than the bandwidth σ.
